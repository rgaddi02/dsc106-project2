Project 2 Persuasive or Deceptive Visualization?

Andy Cai, Rei Gaddi, Raymond Guo — adcai@ucsd.edu, rgaddi@ucsd.edu, r8guo@ucsd.edu

The NYC Civilian Complaint Review Board is more likely to substantiate allegations of abuse of authority than allegations of excessive force.

Data: Civilian Complaints Against New York City Police Officers, compiled by ProPublica from NYC Civilian Complaint Review Board (CCRB) records. Each row is one allegation; the dataset includes the complaint ID, the FADO category (Force, Abuse of Authority, Discourtesy, Offensive Language), and the board’s disposition.

FOR the Proposition

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • Score: -1.5. Counting allegations instead of complaints. We computed the substantiation rate per allegation: for each FADO type, the share of allegations whose board disposition was “Substantiated” (any discipline level). A single complaint often contains several allegations, and force allegations tend to come bundled with abuse-of-authority ones in the same incident, so this unit of analysis quietly decides the result. This was the most important and least visible choice we made. A reader sees an ordinary percentage and has no reason to ask what was counted, and the Against chart shows that counting complaints instead almost erases the gap. We considered showing both units side by side but dropped the idea because it would weaken the argument.
  • Score: -1. Showing only two of the four allegation categories. The dataset also records Discourtesy and Offensive Language allegations. Leaving them out turns the chart into a head-to-head contest, which makes the proposition feel like the obvious reading. Including all four would have put the 30.4% next to other categories and made it look less exceptional. Because the proposition only names these two categories, the omission looks like focus rather than filtering.
  • Score: -0.5. Ratio framing (“more than twice as likely”, “2.4 times as often”). Both the title and the subtitle describe the gap as a multiplier instead of as the absolute difference of 17.6 percentage points. Ratios between small percentages sound dramatic, and “twice as likely” is far more memorable than “18 points.” The ratio is arithmetically correct, so we did not score this lower. Still, we picked it because it sounds bigger, not because it is the most informative summary. We also tried “Abuse of authority is upheld far more often,” but a specific number reads as more authoritative than an adjective.
  • Score: -0.5. Faded color and unbolded label on the force bar. The abuse-of-authority bar is drawn in a saturated blue with a bold label, and the force bar is pale with regular-weight text. This visual hierarchy pushes the reader to read force as the “weak” category before they even compare the numbers. It is a common, defensible highlighting technique, but here it reinforces a conclusion rather than just helping navigation. A neutral alternative would have given both bars the same color.
  • Score: +0.5. Zero-based axis ending at 40%. The axis starts at zero, so the bar lengths stay proportional to the values. Ending it at 40% instead of 100% lets the larger bar fill most of the frame, which makes the comparison feel more dramatic without distorting the ratio. It also avoids an obvious truncated-axis trick that a skeptical reader would catch right away and that would hurt the chart’s credibility.

AGAINST the Proposition

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • Score: -1.5. Grouping allegations into complaints. We grouped allegations by complaint ID and flagged a complaint as “upheld” if any of its allegations was substantiated. We then compared complaints containing an abuse-of-authority allegation with complaints containing a force allegation. As a result, a force complaint counts as upheld even when only a different, non-force allegation in it was substantiated. That is exactly what pulls the force rate up from 12.8% to 37.3%. The subtitle states the metric (“substantiated at least one allegation”), so the choice is technically disclosed. However, few readers will notice that it no longer measures how force allegations themselves are judged.
  • Score: -1. Overlapping comparison groups. The two groups are not mutually exclusive: a complaint alleging both force and abuse of authority appears in both bars. Since so many force complaints also include abuse-of-authority allegations, the two rates are partly computed from the same complaints, which pushes them toward each other. The label “Complaints that also allege force” hints at this overlap while making it sound like a natural comparison. Mutually exclusive groups (force-only vs. authority-only) would have been more honest, but they would also have produced a less convenient number.
  • Score: -1. Causal title. “Adding a force allegation doesn’t change a complaint’s odds” describes the data as if we had run an experiment that added an allegation to otherwise identical complaints. In reality, we are comparing two different, self-selected sets of complaints. The causal phrasing is more persuasive than a descriptive title such as “Complaints with force allegations are substantiated at similar rates.” Because it sounds measured rather than charged, it slips past the reader. We avoided stronger language such as “myth” or “debunked” because it would read as an obvious slant.
  • Score: +1. Full 0–100% axis with direct labels and a stated difference. We showed the whole percentage range, labeled each bar with its exact value, and wrote out the 0.5-point gap. Taken alone, this is a transparent and conventional design. With both bars near 38%, the empty space to the right makes the difference look trivial, and the explicit “0.5 percentage points” annotation gives readers a concrete number to remember. This choice is earnest in itself; its persuasive power comes from the transformation behind it.
  • Score: +0.5. Reversing the color emphasis. This time the force bar is the saturated one. That draws the eye to the category the proposition is about and shows it standing level with abuse of authority. Using the same palette as the For chart, with the emphasis swapped, keeps the design looking consistent and neutral.

Final Reflection

After our exploratory analysis, the visual side of persuasion was straightforward. Emphasizing one bar with saturated color and bold labels, or choosing where an axis ends, took only a few minutes and immediately changed how the charts “felt.” The harder part was finding an honest-looking way to argue against a gap that clearly exists in the allegation-level data. A truncated axis or a hidden outlier would have been easy to spot, so we needed a framing that stays internally consistent and still tells a different story. The difficulty was not making a chart look a certain way; it was finding a defensible question whose answer happened to support our side.

What surprised us most is that the biggest persuasive choices were not visual at all. Both charts use nearly the same plain, conventional design: zero-based horizontal bars, direct labels, a cited source, and a matching palette. Neither contains a classic “lie factor.” Yet one shows a 2.4× difference and the other shows a gap of 0.5 percentage points, and the only real difference is whether we counted allegations or complaints, and whether the comparison groups overlap. These decisions happened in pandas before any chart was drawn, and a reader cannot see them by inspecting the image. The conventional styling actually helped both charts seem more trustworthy, which echoes Kennedy et al.’s point that visualization conventions signal objectivity whether or not it is deserved.

We now think of ethical analysis and visualization as being less about avoiding specific chart tricks and more about whether the reader could reconstruct, and would agree with, the question the chart is answering. The line we would draw is this: a choice is acceptable persuasion when it emphasizes a real pattern under a definition the reader can see and would accept as fair, such as highlighting a bar, choosing a clear title, or using the full axis. It becomes misleading when it depends on the reader not knowing a choice was made, such as switching the unit of analysis, using overlapping groups, or implying causation from observational data. That line is fuzzy, because the same technique (a ratio, an emphasis color, a title) can fall on either side depending on context. In practice, the most useful test we found was to ask whether the chart would still persuade if its methodology were printed directly beneath it. Under that test, both of our charts fall short in places, and seeing that clearly was the main lesson of this project.
